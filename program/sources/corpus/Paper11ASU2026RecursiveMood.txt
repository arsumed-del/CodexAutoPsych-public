       A Minimal Recursive Loop Model of Mood and
                   Anxiety Disorders

                                                 Arthur Su
                                        ORCID: 0009-0002-5678-4708



                                                  Abstract

          This paper proposes a minimal conceptual recursive loop model of depression, anxiety, and
      mania. Within the model, pathology is defined as recursive gain exceeding corrective constraint.
      Under this shared condition, depression can be conceptualized as negative recursion grounded
      in the past, anxiety as negative recursion projected into the future, and mania as positive or
      expansive recursion. The model builds on repetitive negative thinking, rumination, worry, and
      positive rumination literatures, but compresses these constructs into a deliberately rudimentary
      conceptual and clinical heuristic. The framework is not intended to replace diagnostic, biological,
      or psychodynamic accounts, but to offer a compact formal scaffold for thinking about depressive,
      anxious, and manic attractor states.



1    Introduction

Diagnostic categories name symptom clusters, but clinical states may also be understood as re-
cursive loops. In the cognitive and clinical psychology literature, repetitive negative thinking has
been described as a transdiagnostic process that cuts across diagnostic boundaries, with rumination
and worry sharing a process structure while differing in content and temporal orientation (Ehring
and Watkins, 2008; Watkins, 2008). Rumination has been strongly linked to depression, including
its onset, severity, and duration (Nolen-Hoeksema et al., 2008), while worry has classically been
characterized by anxious apprehension and concern over future events (Borkovec et al., 1983).
This paper proposes that these constructs can be formalized as minimal valence-time loops. De-
pression can be conceptualized as negative recursion grounded in the past, whereas anxiety can be
conceptualized as negative recursion projected into the future. This formulation does not claim
that all depressive content is explicitly past-tense or that all anxious content is grammatically
future-tense. Rather, the temporal axis is intended to track the evidentiary substrate of the loop:
depressive recursion often appears to draw on accumulated loss, shame, guilt, failure, or perceived
defectiveness, whereas anxious recursion often appears to draw on anticipated threat, uncertainty,
or future overwhelm.
A parallel literature on positive affective responses in bipolar disorder suggests a possible extension
of this model to mania. Individuals with bipolar disorder may ruminate in response to positive
affect, and measures of positive rumination capture self-focused and emotion-focused amplification
of positive affect (Johnson et al., 2008; Feldman et al., 2008). Rumination about positive emotion
has also been associated with bipolar illness course, including lifetime mania frequency (Gruber

et al., 2011). These findings suggest that mania may be modeled as a positive or expansive recursive
loop, rather than simply as elevated mood.
The present paper therefore proposes a minimal recursive loop model with three signatures:


                                       Depression ∼ V − T − ,


                                         Anxiety ∼ V − T + ,


                                           Mania ∼ V + .

These expressions are intended to describe the valence-time signature of each state. They are taken
to be pathological only under the shared condition:


                                              G > K,

where G denotes recursive gain and K denotes corrective constraint. In compact form: depression
can be thought of as negative-past recursion, anxiety as negative-future recursion, and mania as
positive recursion. Within this model, pathology begins when recursive gain exceeds corrective
constraint.
The model is intended as a conceptual scaffold rather than a validated mechanistic or diagnostic
framework.


2    Shared Pathology Condition

Within this framework, all three loops are taken to become pathological only when recursive gain
exceeds corrective constraint:


                                              G > K.

Recursive gain refers to the degree to which a loop increasingly uses itself as evidence. Corrective
constraint refers to the forces that may interrupt, contextualize, or regulate the loop, including
metacognition, external feedback, sleep, behavioral evidence, social correction, reality testing, ex-
ecutive regulation, and environmental structure.
A negative past thought is not, by itself, depression. A future worry is not, by itself, anxiety.
Confidence is not, by itself, mania. The loop is treated as pathological only when it becomes
self-amplifying and insufficiently corrected.
The loop expressions describe the valence-time signature of each state; the pathology condition
G > K is assumed throughout. Figure 1 shows the general schematic: affective content enters a
recursive operator that iterates and accumulates gain, with the outcome determined by whether G
exceeds K.

3     Core Variables

3.1   Valence

Negative valence, denoted V − , is used here to refer to negative affective meaning. It includes loss,
guilt, shame, fear, threat, failure, helplessness, and perceived diminishment.
Positive or expansive valence, denoted V + , is used to refer to affective meaning organized around
possibility, agency, confidence, mission, significance, specialness, or expansion.


3.2   Time

Past-oriented evidentiary substrate is denoted T − . Future-oriented evidentiary substrate is denoted
T +.
The temporal axis is intended to track the evidentiary substrate of the loop, not merely the surface
grammar of the patient’s report. For example, “I am worthless” may sound present-tense, but
clinically it often appears to rest on retrospective evidence: accumulated shame, failure, loss,
rejection, or perceived defectiveness. Similarly, “I cannot handle anything” may sound trait-like,
but often appears to point toward anticipated future demands, failure, or overwhelm.


4     Depression: Negative-Past Recursion

                                           Depression = V − T − ,

Depression can be conceptualized as negative recursion grounded in the past. This may correspond

5    Anxiety: Negative-Future Recursion

                                         Anxiety = V − T + ,

Anxiety can be conceptualized as negative recursion projected into the future. This may correspond
clinically to worry and catastrophizing, in which negative affective meaning is repeatedly projected
into anticipated threat, uncertainty, or future inability to cope (Borkovec et al., 1983; Ehring and
Watkins, 2008).
Its core loop may be described as:


                   future threat → arousal → avoidance → more future threat.

A representative narrative form might be:


      The future may harm me.


The recursive operator can be written as:

                                       n
                                      Rcatastrophizing (V − T + ).

Common clinical motifs include threat, uncertainty, catastrophe, danger, hypervigilance, avoidance,
reassurance seeking, and somatic arousal.

6    Mania: Positive Expansive Recursion

                                             Mania = V + .

Mania can be conceptualized as positive or expansive recursion. Unlike depression and anxiety as
modeled here, mania does not appear to be bound to a single temporal direction. It may involve
reinterpretation of the past as destiny and projection of the future as mission. This formulation is
consistent with work on positive rumination in bipolar disorder, including self-focused and emotion-
focused responses to positive affect (Johnson et al., 2008; Feldman et al., 2008; Gruber et al., 2011).
A possible past expression:


      My whole life was leading to this.


A possible future expression:


      Everything is possible now.


A unified manic narrative might take the form:


      My past, present, and future confirm my significance.


Its core loop may be described as:


             significance → confidence → action → reinforcement → more significance.

The recursive operator can be written as:

                                      n                            +
                                     Rsignificance amplification (V ).


Common clinical motifs include grandiosity, specialness, mission, destiny, possibility, confidence,
energy, reduced sleep, and increased goal-directed behavior.


7    Structural Asymmetry

A structural asymmetry follows from the model: negative loops appear partitioned by temporal
substrate, whereas the positive loop is not similarly partitioned. Figure 2 illustrates this asymmetry.
Depression and anxiety occupy localized regions in the negative-valence half of the valence-time
plane, separated by temporal substrate. Mania, as modeled here, is not constrained to one temporal
direction.

Anxiety and mania may both involve forward projection, but anxiety appears to anticipate threat
while mania appears to amplify significance. Existing repetitive negative thinking literature sup-
ports the distinction between rumination and worry as related negative repetitive processes with
different content and temporal emphases (Ehring and Watkins, 2008; Watkins, 2008). Positive
rumination literature provides one empirical basis for considering manic amplification as a parallel
high-gain process involving positive or expansive affective meaning (Johnson et al., 2008; Gruber
et al., 2011).


8    Treatment Implications

Within this framework, treatment may be understood as targeting the shared pathology condition:


                                                  6
                                                G↓

or:


                                               K↑.

Different treatment modalities may act on the same loop through different mechanisms: phar-
macology, psychotherapy, sleep restoration, behavioral activation, exposure, social structure, and
environmental change can all be understood as attempts to reduce G, increase K, or both.
In this framing, treatment for depression may be understood as reducing rumination and increasing
agency, activity, reward, social feedback, and alternative self-evidence.
Treatment for anxiety may be understood as reducing catastrophizing and avoidance while increas-
ing exposure, uncertainty tolerance, somatic regulation, and corrective learning.
Treatment for mania may be understood as reducing significance amplification while increasing
sleep, inhibition, external structure, mood stabilization, and reality constraint.


9     Discussion

The model is intentionally minimal and conceptual. Its purpose is not to replace categorical diag-
nosis, neurobiology, or psychotherapy models, but to offer a compact clinical scaffold for thinking
about affective attractor states. It builds on repetitive thinking literature by formalizing rumina-
tion, worry, and positive rumination as recursive loops that may be differentiated by valence and
temporal substrate.
Within a broader dynamical-systems frame, the present model may be read as a low-dimensional
content map for affective attractor space. Where Scheffer et al. (2024) describe psychiatric disor-
ders as alternative stable states and Kheirkhah et al. (2025) frame treatment as control input on
neurobehavioral manifolds, the present model offers three recurring loop signatures—depression,
anxiety, and mania—as a candidate minimal content theory for what may populate that affective
manifold.
A limited neuroscience bridge may also be drawn. For example, depressive rumination has been
linked to default mode network and subgenual prefrontal connectivity, suggesting one possible
neural correlate of negative self-referential recursion (Hamilton et al., 2015). This paper does
not attempt to build a full network neuroscience model, but the recursive loop framework may
later be expanded to include large-scale network configuration, monoaminergic control parameters,
defensive transformations, attachment dynamics, and psychosis as constraint failure.


10     Limitations

This model does not attempt to fully account for mixed states, irritable mania, psychosis, trauma,
obsessive-compulsive disorder, personality pathology, attachment dynamics, defense mechanisms,

                                                 7
network neuroscience, or monoamine control parameters.
It also intentionally compresses complex clinical phenomena into a small formal vocabulary. This
is both a strength and a limitation. The model is designed for conceptual simplification and for
thinking about pathological psychological attractor states that may partially map onto current
clinical diagnostic clusters; it is not intended as a comprehensive mechanistic explanation, and the
mappings proposed here are heuristic rather than validated.
Finally, grief, caution, and ambition are not treated here as pathological. Within the model, they
would become pathological only when:


                                               G > K.


11     Conclusion

Depression, anxiety, and mania can be conceptualized as three recursive affective loops under a
shared pathology condition. Depression can be thought of as negative-past recursion. Anxiety
can be thought of as negative-future recursion. Mania can be thought of as positive or expansive
recursion. Within the model, pathology begins when recursive gain exceeds corrective constraint.
The framework is offered as a heuristic scaffold for thinking about affective attractor states, rather
than as a comprehensive or validated mechanistic account.
